01 / BASICS

Meet the instrument in your hands

Six strings and a dozen frets look complicated, but you only need two things: which string is which note, and where the left hand presses while the right hand picks. Let's name the parts first.

Parts of the guitar

Tap a number · or hover over a part

Six strings — learn these first

Click to hear

Standard tuning, thick to thin, is E A D G B E. Strings are numbered from the thinnest: the thinnest is the 1st string, the thickest is the 6th.

Mnemonic: Eat All Day, Get Big Easy — from the 6th string to the 1st.

Three words you'll use every day

Fret
The space between the metal bars on the neck. The open string is fret 0; the first space is fret 1.
Note names
C D E F G A B, also sung as do re mi fa sol la si.
Chord
Several strings pressed and sounded together. Most pop songs are mostly chords.

Steel-string acoustics are bright and will hurt your fingertips at first; nylon-string classicals are softer. Start on steel if you want to sing along — the soreness fades in about two weeks.

02 / BASICS

Dead notes, sore hands, no speed — it's usually posture

Posture isn't a performance of “correct form”. It decides whether you can relax, make notes ring, and move quickly. Lock in these two hands in the first two weeks and everything later gets easier.

ASitting: three points of contact

  1. Rest the waist of the guitar on your right thigh, with the body against yours.
  2. Angle the soundboard slightly up. The headstock should sit around shoulder height — don't stare down at the fretboard.
  3. Let your right forearm rest on the top edge of the body, but don't press the soundboard dead.
  4. Keep your back naturally upright. The guitar leans on you; you don't clamp it.

Standing? Use a strap and set the guitar to the same height as when you sit. Practise sitting, perform standing — keep the feel the same.

BLeft hand: fingertips, arch, close to the fret

  1. Thumb on the centre of the back of the neck, opposite the index finger. Don't wrap your whole hand around it.
  2. Arch the first knuckle and press with the fingertip, not the pad.
  3. Press 1–2 mm to the left of the fret wire (toward the headstock) — not on the metal, not in the middle of the space.
  4. Push the wrist slightly forward to give your fingers room.

If a note won't ring, check in this order: position → pressure → a finger touching the neighbouring string.

Two millimetres make a big difference

Side view

CRight hand: the wrist does the work

  • Hold the pick between thumb and index with 2–3 mm showing. Don't squeeze.
  • Angle the pick 30–45° to the string. Downstroke with the tip, upstroke with the back edge.
  • The wrist moves, not the whole arm. Imagine flicking water off your hand.
  • Fingerstyle: thumb covers strings 6/5/4; index 3, middle 2, ring 1.

DFour common mistakes

Flat fingersThe pad mutes the next string. Stand the fingers up.
Pressing on the fretNo length of string is left to vibrate; the note thuds. Move 2 mm left.
Thumb wrapped overYour fingers can't stretch and changes get slow. Put the thumb back behind the neck.
Collapsed wristYou can't reach the low strings and your hand cramps. Push the wrist forward.

03 / SETUP

Tune before you play. Every time.

If the guitar is out of tune, practice makes your ear worse — it memorises the wrong pitch. Standard pitch is A4 = 440 Hz. Thick to thin: E2 · A2 · D3 · G3 · B3 · E4.

Tuning in five steps

  1. Start on the 6th string. Pluck it gently and let it ring.
  2. Watch the needle: flat → tighten slowly; sharp → loosen.
  3. Turn the peg no more than a quarter turn at a time, listening as you go.
  4. When the needle sits in the green and holds for two seconds, move to the next string.
  5. After all six, check the 6th again — tuning one string changes the neck tension.

New strings drift for the first three days. That's normal. Tune every time you pick up the guitar; after a week they'll settle.

No tuner? Tune by ear

If one string is already in tune, use it as a reference, one string at a time. “Hear the pair” plays the reference note, then the target open string.

The intervals: 6th string, 5th fret = open 5th; 5th string, 5th fret = open 4th; 4th string, 5th fret = open 3rd; 3rd string, 4th fret = open 2nd; 2nd string, 5th fret = open 1st.

Two safety notes Turn the pegs slowly; never more than half a turn at once. Pluck gently when tuning. A string can snap, especially when it's new.

04 / SINGLE NOTES

First, make every note ring clearly

Don't rush into chords. Use single notes to get “left hand presses accurately + right hand picks cleanly” working. Then chords come much faster. The fretboard below is live — click anywhere.

Where the major scale comes from

Two neighbouring frets are a half step; skipping one is a whole step. The major scale formula is: whole · whole · half · whole · whole · whole · half.

Learn the formula and you can build a major scale on any string without memorising dots.

Right hand: make the picking steady

  • Pick straight into the string at a slight 30° angle — the cleanest tone.
  • Downstroke (↓) with the tip; upstroke (↑) with the back edge.
  • At slow tempos use all downstrokes for even tone; alternate when comfortable.
  • Fingerstyle: thumb on the low strings; index, middle and ring on 3, 2, 1.

The test: close your eyes. Twelve notes should sound like one straight line — no note popping out.

05 / CHORDS

Eight chords, half a songbook

Most pop songs live in a handful of chords. The goal isn't “knowing the shape” — it's making it ring first time without looking.

Reading a chord diagram

Vertical: 6th string left → 1st right
  • The thick line at the top is the nut (fret 0); each line below is a fret.
  • Dots show where the fingers go. The number inside is the finger: 1 index, 2 middle, 3 ring, 4 pinky.
  • ○ open string, play it; × don't play it, keep the right hand away.
  • Left to right is 6 5 4 3 2 1, matching the string names underneath.

The order is always: place the fingers → check each string → then strum. Never strum first.

06 / RHYTHM

Steady rhythm is worth more than hard chords

Most people's strumming sounds bad not because of the hand shape, but because the beat wanders. Get “one strum per beat” into your muscle memory first, then add decoration.

Four patterns, simple to fancy

Click a card to hear it; the arrows light up below. Air-strum along before you touch the strings.

Wrist, not arm

Strumming is a small arc from the wrist; the forearm barely moves. Move the arm and you lose speed and hit the wrong strings.

Keep the hand moving

Even when you don't hit the strings, keep the hand swinging in time. Stopping is the number one cause of a late next beat.

Light before loud

Volume comes from wrist travel, not force. Hitting harder causes fret buzz and broken strings, and sounds smaller.

Practice order (don't skip)

  1. 60 BPM, downstrokes only, one per beat, two minutes without drifting.
  2. Move to 80 BPM, still downstrokes; check the wrist hasn't stiffened.
  3. Learn “down, down-up, up-down-up” in the air first, then on the strings.
  4. Loop C → G → Am → F, one bar each, changing slowly.
  5. Play along to a three-minute song all the way through before adding speed.

    How to learn a song (in this order)

    1. Section it: work on 2–4 bars at a time, not the whole song.
    2. Slow it down: 60–70% tempo, slow enough that you don't miss.
    3. Loop it: ten clean passes of the same section builds muscle memory.
    4. Join it: once each section works alone, connect them.
    5. Record it: one phone recording and you'll hear the problem instantly.

    Wrong notes aren't the danger; practising them at speed is. Slow practice is the only shortcut.

    Buying your first guitar

    Read this before you shop
    BudgetCA$200–400 is plenty. Feel matters more than the brand — don't pay for a logo.
    SizeA 41-inch dreadnought suits most adults; smaller hands can choose a 40-inch or OM body.
    ActionAsk the shop to set the action at 2.5–3 mm at the 12th fret. Easy enough to press, or you won't keep practising.
    Try itHave someone play it in the shop. Listen, check the neck is straight, and buy online only with returns.

    Don't buy a toy guitar. Action that's too high makes practice painful enough to quit — that's not a “practice harder” problem.

    Self-test: did it stick?

    Find-the-note trains the fretboard; ear training trains your ear. Don't chase a perfect score — use “Hear it again”.

    Four questions every beginner asks

    My notes won't ring, or buzz. What do I check?

    In order: ① is the finger 1–2 mm to the left of the fret wire; ② is it upright, with the pad clear of the neighbouring string; ③ is there enough pressure. Most dead notes are position, not strength.

    Is fingertip pain normal?

    For the first two weeks, yes — calluses form and it stops. But sharp joint pain means stop and rest a day. Two 10-minute sessions beat one long one, and hurt less.

    How do I change chords faster?

    Three tricks: ① keep common fingers down between chords; ② move the lowest note first so the strum starts right; ③ look ahead to the next shape. Then use a metronome and change every four beats, slow enough to keep up.

    Theory first, or playing first?

    Both, but playing leads. For each chord, know the three notes it contains; for each scale, know where it sits on the neck. Reading theory without touching the guitar is very slow progress.

    ONE LAST THING

    Slow is fast.

    Make every note clean and count every beat. Time does the rest. In two weeks you'll have a song you can play all the way through.
